## What I would do differently in my next talk

Serendipitously, through a Simon Willison post this week, I found and read his earlier [post](https://simonwillison.net/2019/Dec/10/better-presentations/) on better presentations from way back when (2019).

Some of the things that stood out to him from a course he took:

> - Every talk should start with an AIM: Audience, Intent, Message. Who are the audience for the talk? What do you intend to achieve by giving the presentation? With those two things in mind, you can construct the message—the actual content of the talk.
> - Try to include at least one STAR moment—Something They’ll Always Remember. This can be a gimmick, a repeated theme, a well-selected video or audio clip. Something to help the talk stand out.
> - Presentations are most interesting if they are structured with contrasts. These can be emotional high and low points, or content that illustrates what _is_ compared to what _could be_. [Sparklines](https://www.duarte.com/presentation-skills-resources/creating-moments-of-impact-using-sparklines-for-strategic-conversations/) are a tool that can be used to think about this structure.
> - The human brain is incredibly attuned to stories. If you can find an excuse to tell a story, no matter how thin that excuse is, take it.

I have been thinking about presentations and talks as well, perhaps because I gave [one](/nordletter/nl124-speaking-gigs) recently, but more pertinently, because I have seen and heard so many people give talks now that I have an opinion.

My list of [best practices for presenting things](/evergreen/best-practices-for-presenting-things) is thus:

1. Less text on the slide. Just pointers not descriptions. A person viewing should be able to skim quickly. This is not a SOP document.
2. Have a demo. Demos are fun.
3. If you include diagrams, they should be visible, i.e. the text should be visible.
4. Consistent design language. Don’t jump from one style to a different one. I prefer minimalist styles.
5. Pick a topic based on the audience. A super technical presentation will be difficult to pull off. Generally speaking, it’s easier to do overview sort of talks. But if the audience is expecting a deep dive, do not be afraid to do so.

## Ghost-Eye

![Ghosts](https://storage.sajalchoudhary.net/images/2026/10/nl125-03.jpeg)

I finished reading [Ghost-Eye](/bookshelf/ghost-eye) this week. I had known of Amitav Ghosh from before, from books whispered here and there, like a book on climate change he had written recently - a book I have not read yet. So, when I saw Ghost-Eye pop up in the e-library, I immediately put it on hold.

I am not a Bengali, so I can’t say that it took me back to my childhood days in Calcutta. And yet, it did. It took me back to the streets of J&K block in Laxmi Nagar in Delhi, where I used to live as a child. It took me back to the time I stood in the street and heard an aunty who lived in the building opposite to our own, tell us tales about her village and the spirits that lived there.

This is a book about reincarnation, but also the world we are living in, about cooking (fish specifically) but also the art of cooking. And everything ties off beautifully by the end - the aha moment.

I loved reading it. The characters are so well written. Give it a read.

## Like a river

![Changing weather](https://storage.sajalchoudhary.net/images/2026/10/nl125-02.jpeg)

I feel overwhelmed by the amount of stuff that I need to read. It was the same reason I tried [current](/blog/the-problem-with-current). I did not like the fact that it took the control away from me, but I liked the concept of it.

[Oliver Burkeman talks about this in his post here.](https://www.oliverburkeman.com/river)

> To return to information overload: this means treating your "to read" pile like a river (a stream that flows past you, and from which you pluck a few choice items, here and there) instead of a bucket (which demands that you empty it). After all, you presumably don't feel overwhelmed by all the unread books in the British Library – and not because there aren't an overwhelming number of them, but because it never occurred to you that it might be your job to get through them all.

This provides the same mental model - something that flows and you just pick what interests you. I have a similar setup on my website (but from a writing context) - between [garden](https://sajalchoudhary.net/garden/) and [stream](https://sajalchoudhary.net/stream/).

Some things feel obvious *after* you read them. This feels like one of those things.
